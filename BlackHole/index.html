Side project

About the black hole.

It's a real-time raymarcher I wrote to render gravitational lensing from first principles — no library, no asset, no shortcut. I built it to understand how light actually behaves around mass. Below: how it works, and why each piece is there.

A.Problem

I wanted to render a convincing black hole — not a shader-toy approximation, but something where every feature depended on light actually bending. If the image looks right, the math is right; the picture is its own proof.

B.Technical challenge

Gravitational lensing cannot be a screen-space warp. Photons follow curved paths, so the far side of the disk must lens up and over the shadow and self-occlude. That rules out meshes and textures — it forces a per-pixel raymarcher integrating a curved path.

C.Architecture

One fullscreen GLSL fragment shader, no geometry. Each pixel reconstructs a world ray from Three.js's inverse projection, marches it (bending each step toward the singularity), accumulates disk emission on plane crossings, and samples a procedural starfield in the deflected direction so the background lenses for free.

D.Performance

The naïve march is hundreds of millions of ops per frame. Held within budget by an adaptive step size (fine near the horizon, coarse far out), early-out when a ray escapes, a hard 240-step ceiling, transmittance termination, and a device-pixel-ratio cap.

E.Decisions

Newtonian-style deflection rather than a full Schwarzschild geodesic — visually indistinguishable at this scale, several times cheaper. Disk emission is integrated inside the same pass (the only way to lens and self-occlude it correctly). Doppler beaming softened so the receding side still reads as moving.

F.What's next

Replace the Newtonian deflection with a true Kerr geodesic integrator (the src/shaders/kerr.glsl draft is the start). Add a click-to-orbit camera, and a "physically accurate vs cinematic" toggle so visitors can see the difference for themselves.

The pipeline · one fragment shader, per pixel

  1. 01

    Ray reconstruction

    Each pixel's screen UV is turned back into a world-space ray using Three.js's inverse projection — so the march sees exactly the camera the page thinks it has.

  2. 02

    Curved-space march

    The ray is integrated in small steps; each step its direction bends toward the singularity (Δdir ∝ −pos/|pos|³). Step size is adaptive — fine near the horizon, coarse far out.

  3. 03

    Volumetric shroud

    Inside ~3.3× the horizon radius, 3D noise accumulates into a churning gas bank that veils the silhouette — so the shadow is never a hard black cutout.

  4. 04

    Disk emission

    When the bent ray crosses the tilted equatorial plane, Doppler-beamed accretion light is added — approaching side bright and blue, receding side dim and red.

  5. 05

    Capture, stars & photon ring

    Rays that fall inside the horizon are captured — the shadow is their absence. Survivors sample a procedural starfield in the deflected direction, plus a closest-approach photon-ring term.

  6. 06

    Post & output

    ACES filmic tone-mapping, an UnrealBloom pass for the disk's glow, and a triangular-PDF dither that erases 8-bit banding across the dark gradients.
