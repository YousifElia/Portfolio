01  Featured · Case Study

Black Hole Raymarcher

The black hole behind this page is not a video or a library. It is a real-time raymarcher I wrote to render gravitational lensing from first principles — and the clearest single piece of evidence on this site.

A.Problem

A portfolio hero is usually a stock image or a particle field. I wanted the first thing a visitor sees to be proof — something that can't be downloaded. A black hole is the hardest "simple" object to render honestly: every feature depends on light actually bending, so a convincing image is the evidence.

B.Technical Challenge

Real gravitational lensing cannot be a screen-space warp. Photons follow curved paths, so the far side of the accretion disk must lens up and over the shadow and self-occlude. That rules out meshes and textures — it forces a per-pixel raymarcher integrating a curved path.

C.Rendering Architecture

One fullscreen GLSL fragment shader, no geometry. Each pixel reconstructs a world ray from Three.js's inverse projection, then marches it: bending toward the singularity each step, accumulating disk emission on plane crossings, sampling a procedural starfield in the deflected direction so the background lenses for free.

D.Performance Constraints

A naïve raymarch is hundreds of millions of operations per frame. The budget is held with an adaptive step size (fine near the horizon, coarse far away), early-out once a ray escapes, a hard 240-step ceiling, transmittance termination, and a device-pixel-ratio cap.

E.Engineering Decisions

Newtonian-style deflection instead of a full Schwarzschild geodesic — visually indistinguishable at this scale and several times cheaper. Disk emission is integrated inside the same pass, the only way to lens and self-occlude it correctly. Doppler beaming is softened so the receding side still reads as moving.

F.Measurable Outcomes

A locked 60fps on integrated laptop graphics in a single draw call, ~270 lines of GLSL, zero texture memory, and a renderer that pauses itself when off-screen or backgrounded — so the spectacle never costs the battery.

03  About

Who's behind the renderer

I'm a second-year computer science student at the University of Windsor. I'm drawn to problems where the interesting part is the design itself — a numerical integration scheme, the shape of a data model, the architecture of a system — not just getting code to compile.

I learn by rebuilding things from first principles. The black hole on this page isn't a library I imported; it's a raymarcher I wrote to understand how light actually behaves around mass. That's the throughline in everything here: understand the why, then build the thing.
